Economic Voting

Abstract

Voters in developed democracies typically experience considerable variation in economic outcomes prior to an election. Our theory suggests that voters receive signals that allow them to tease out the portion of that variance which results from exogenous shocks as opposed to competence shocks, although the strength of these signals will likely vary across contexts. We believe that the international economy provides a key signal that voters can use to help distinguish competence from exogenous economic shocks. In the context of the international economy variation in macro-economic outcomes can be comparatively quite substantial or quite small. We argue that the former case sends a signal to the voter that domestic economic outcomes are not simply the result of global economic shocks beyond the control of incumbent governments. Hence domestic economic outcomes that vary significantly from the international mean raise the voter’s assessment of the incumbent’s competence. This in turn is hypothesized to increase the extent to which voters hold incumbent coalitions responsible for economic outcomes. In this paper we explore strategies for assessing the relative magnitude of within-country versus cross-national variations in economic outcomes and how this impacts cross-national variations in economic voting. The theoretical foundation for the explanation builds on selection models of vote choice in which voters treat economic outcomes as a competency signal and context matters because it systematically affects the nature of the competency signals voters receive. Empirical tests of these arguments are based on a very unique data base consisting of 165 elections studies conducted in 18 different countries over a 20 year time period (1980-2001) along with monthly measures of economic outcomes over the same period. The authors also propose a set of novel estimation strategies for dealing with the multi-level estimation issues associated with the datasets.
